If you're a spray foam contractor who bought general liability insurance from a generalist agent, there's a good chance your policy has at least one exclusion that would deny your most likely claim. I see it constantly. Not because agents are dishonest — but because most of them don't know what they don't know about spray foam.

Here's what I look for in every policy I review, and why each of these exclusions matters specifically for spray foam work.

## 1. The Faulty Workmanship Exclusion (And Why Off-Ratio Makes It Worse)

Every GL policy has a faulty workmanship exclusion. Standard language excludes property damage that results from faulty, defective, or inadequate workmanship. For most trades, this exclusion is straightforward — if you build something wrong, GL doesn't pay to rebuild it correctly.

But here's where spray foam gets complicated.

When foam goes off-ratio — when the A-component (isocyanate) and B-component (polyol blend) mix at the wrong ratio — it doesn't just produce bad foam in that moment. It creates a product that looks correct on the surface but fails progressively. Six months later, a homeowner notices sagging insulation, structural softening, or odor issues. They file a claim. Your carrier looks at the loss and sees: faulty workmanship. Denied.

The fix is a policy that includes properly written products-completed operations coverage with language specific to off-ratio foam scenarios. Some surplus lines markets have endorsements designed for exactly this. Standard admitted markets rarely do.

## 2. Products-Completed Operations Coverage

PCO coverage is supposed to protect you after the job is done — when the work leaves your hands and something goes wrong later. Most GL policies include PCO in the aggregate, but whether it actually responds to a spray foam claim depends on the specific exclusion language in the policy.

The problems arise when:

- The policy has a broad faulty workmanship exclusion that overrides PCO
- The PCO aggregate is separate from and much lower than the GL aggregate
- The policy excludes your specific class of work from PCO

When I place spray foam GL, I'm looking for PCO coverage that explicitly holds up for completed spray foam installations, with an aggregate that reflects the scale of your work. A $1 million PCO aggregate sounds like a lot until you have a large commercial job where a failed foam application damages $800,000 worth of building components and contents.

## 3. The Pollution Exclusion

This is the one that surprises contractors most.

Standard GL policies have an absolute pollution exclusion that bars coverage for bodily injury or property damage arising from the release, discharge, or dispersal of pollutants. Carriers routinely define "pollutants" broadly — and the chemicals in spray polyurethane foam systems (isocyanates in particular) can qualify.

What does this mean in practice? If someone on a job site has a chemical exposure reaction to SPF off-gassing during or after application, your GL carrier may deny the bodily injury claim under the pollution exclusion. If chemical odors from a fresh application affect adjacent tenants or neighboring properties, that property damage claim could get denied the same way.

The right fix is either a GL policy written specifically for spray foam that carves out SPF chemical exposures from the pollution exclusion, or a standalone contractor pollution liability (CPL) policy that covers exactly these scenarios. Often you need both.

## 4. The Professional Services Exclusion

Some GL policies — especially package policies marketed to contractors — include a professional services exclusion. The language typically bars coverage for claims arising from any professional service, advice, or design work.

Here's the problem for spray foam contractors: mix ratio selection, ambient temperature assessment, substrate compatibility evaluation — a clever carrier attorney can argue these are professional determinations that triggered the loss. If the policy has a broad professional services exclusion, the claim gets denied on those grounds.

I've seen this exclusion used successfully by carriers on spray foam claims. It's not theoretical. When I review a policy for a spray foam contractor, I'm reading the professional services exclusion language carefully to understand whether it could be weaponized against the most common SPF failures.

## 5. Getting Properly Covered

So what does proper spray foam GL look like?

At minimum, you need:

- **General liability written on a form that explicitly includes spray foam/SPF work** — not just "insulation contractors" as a class code
- **Products-completed operations coverage** with adequate limits and language that holds up for off-ratio and other SPF-specific failure modes
- **Pollution exclusion language reviewed** for SPF chemical exposure scenarios — often supplemented with a CPL policy
- **No broad professional services exclusion** that could be used to deny claims arising from mix ratio or application decisions
- **Adequate per-occurrence and aggregate limits** for the scale of commercial work you're doing

The carriers who write spray foam GL well are mostly in the surplus lines market. They understand the risk. Their forms are designed for it. Their claims adjusters have seen SPF losses before.

If you bought your GL from a standard admitted carrier through a generalist agent, it's worth having someone who knows this trade look at your policy. Not to scare you — but because knowing what you have (and what you don't) is the first step to actually being protected.
