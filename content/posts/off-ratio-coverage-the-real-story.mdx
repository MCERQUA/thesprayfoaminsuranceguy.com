If you've been in spray foam long enough, you've either had an off-ratio job or you know someone who has. The A and B components didn't mix right. The foam looked fine when you sprayed it. A month later the homeowner calls because something smells wrong, the foam is tacky, or there's visible degradation. Now you've got a problem — and whether your insurance covers it is the question that keeps contractors up at night.

I've been placing spray foam insurance for two decades. Off-ratio claims are the most common loss I see in this trade. And the dirty truth is that most GL policies — the ones generalist agents place with standard admitted carriers — don't cover them.

Let me explain why, and what you actually need.

## What Off-Ratio Foam Actually Means

Spray polyurethane foam requires precise mixing of two components: the A-side (isocyanate, typically MDI) and the B-side (a polyol blend with catalysts, surfactants, and blowing agents). The correct mix ratio varies by product and application but is typically around 1:1 by volume. When the ratio drifts outside acceptable tolerances — whether because of equipment calibration issues, temperature problems, hose imbalance, or human error — the chemical reaction doesn't complete correctly.

The result is foam that may look acceptable during application but has compromised physical properties: poor adhesion, reduced R-value, friability, off-gassing, or structural softening. Closed-cell foam that went off-ratio may feel firm initially but delaminate from the substrate over time. Open-cell foam with a bad ratio can produce long-term odor issues from unreacted chemicals.

The claims that result from off-ratio foam are rarely simple. They often involve:

- Complete removal and replacement of the foam in the affected area
- Repair of substrate damage caused by the failed foam or its removal
- Remediation of odor or chemical contamination
- Loss of use or temporary housing for residential customers
- Third-party property damage in commercial applications

A single off-ratio job on a mid-size commercial building can generate a $200,000 to $500,000 claim. I've seen larger.

## Why Most Policies Don't Cover It

Standard GL policies — including most of the contractor packages sold by generalist agents — have a faulty workmanship exclusion. This exclusion bars coverage for property damage arising from defective, faulty, or inadequate workmanship.

Off-ratio foam is almost always classified as faulty workmanship by a carrier's claims adjuster. The mix was wrong. That's the work. The exclusion applies. Claim denied.

Some carriers also use the products-completed operations exclusion to deny off-ratio claims on completed jobs. The argument: the damage isn't from an "occurrence" in the insurance sense; it's from a known-defect product that was always going to fail. Therefore there's no covered occurrence.

I've also seen carriers attempt to use the pollution exclusion on off-ratio claims — particularly when unreacted isocyanate causes off-gassing that affects building occupants. The chemical release gets classified as a pollutant event, and coverage gets denied under the absolute pollution exclusion.

The net result: a contractor who thinks they're insured finds out during a $300,000 claim that their policy has three different exclusions that might apply, and the carrier is using all three.

## What Proper Off-Ratio Coverage Looks Like

Getting proper off-ratio coverage requires working with a carrier that has specifically underwritten the spray foam risk — not a standard contractor GL carrier trying to fit SPF into their existing program.

The right policy will have:

**1. A modified faulty workmanship provision** — not an absolute exclusion, but language that covers property damage resulting from faulty workmanship when the damage extends beyond the contractor's own work. This is sometimes called "your work" coverage. It won't pay to redo the foam itself (that's not what GL is for), but it covers damage to the building, its components, and third-party property that results from the failed foam.

**2. Products-completed operations coverage with SPF-specific language** — confirming that off-ratio foam claims on completed jobs are covered as occurrences, not excluded as known product defects.

**3. Pollution coverage carve-outs** — either within the GL policy or via a standalone contractor pollution liability (CPL) endorsement — that cover chemical exposure claims arising from off-ratio off-gassing.

**4. Adequate aggregate limits** — because off-ratio claims are expensive, I typically recommend spray foam contractors carry at least $2 million general aggregate and $2 million products-completed operations aggregate if they're doing any commercial work.

## The Equipment Angle

One thing often missed in the off-ratio conversation: equipment. Off-ratio events frequently trace back to equipment issues — a proportioner that's out of calibration, a hose with a blocked fluid flow path, temperature sensors that drifted. If the equipment failure can be documented, there may be an equipment breakdown claim alongside the GL claim.

This is why commercial inland marine coverage on your rig and equipment matters — not just for theft or accidental damage, but because a documented equipment malfunction can sometimes shift the claim narrative from "contractor error" to "equipment failure," which plays differently with certain carriers.
