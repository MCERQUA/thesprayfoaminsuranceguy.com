After twenty years placing insurance for spray foam contractors, I see the same mistakes over and over. These aren't exotic edge cases — they're the everyday coverage decisions that leave contractors exposed on their most likely claims. Here are the five I see most often, what they cost, and how to fix them.

---

## Mistake #1: Buying GL From a Generalist Agent

The most common mistake, and the one with the highest potential cost.

Most general liability policies sold to contractors through standard agents are designed for general trade contractors — framers, electricians, plumbers. Spray foam is treated as just another "insulation contractor" class. The underwriter doesn't know or care that SPF involves reactive chemicals, off-ratio risks, and application conditions that produce failure modes no other insulation trade has.

The result: standard exclusions that gut your coverage at the worst moment. Faulty workmanship exclusions that deny off-ratio claims. Pollution exclusions that deny chemical exposure claims. Professional services exclusions that deny mix-ratio decisions. None of these are obvious to the contractor who bought the policy, and the generalist agent who sold it often doesn't know enough to flag them.

**The fix:** Work with an agent who specializes in spray foam. There are surplus lines carriers who have built GL programs specifically for SPF contractors — with proper off-ratio coverage, modified faulty workmanship language, and pollution carve-outs. A specialist agent knows which markets to use and what the policy language needs to say.

---

## Mistake #2: No Off-Ratio Coverage

This deserves its own entry because even contractors who work with specialty agents sometimes skip this piece to save money — and it's the wrong place to cut.

Off-ratio foam is the number one source of claims in this trade. I've written about it extensively, but the core issue is simple: when your A and B components mix incorrectly, you can end up with foam that looks fine on install day and fails progressively over months. By the time the claim arrives, you're looking at complete removal, substrate repair, and potentially odor remediation. On a commercial job, that can be $200,000 or more.

Standard GL policies treat off-ratio failures as faulty workmanship and deny the claim. Proper off-ratio coverage — either through a specialty GL product or an endorsement — changes that outcome.

**The fix:** Confirm with your agent that your GL policy explicitly covers claims arising from off-ratio foam mixing, including property damage to the building beyond the foam itself. If your agent doesn't know what you're talking about, find a new agent.

## Mistake #3: Skipping Contractor Pollution Liability

Most spray foam contractors don't think of themselves as "pollution risks." But the chemicals in SPF systems — particularly isocyanates on the A-side — can trigger the pollution exclusion in a standard GL policy if there's a chemical exposure event.

Scenarios that can become pollution claims: off-gassing during application that affects occupants, chemical migration into an adjacent space, an accidental spill during drum handling, or a blowing agent release. These events can result in bodily injury claims (occupant exposure) and property damage claims (contamination of the building or adjacent properties).

Without contractor pollution liability coverage, these claims hit the pollution exclusion in your GL and get denied. CPL fills that gap. It's also the product that protects you when a client claims your chemicals damaged their finishes, electronics, or HVAC system during or after application.

**The fix:** Add a standalone contractor pollution liability policy or confirm that your GL includes a specialty spray foam endorsement that carves out SPF chemical exposures from the pollution exclusion. CPL limits of $1 million per occurrence are a starting point; larger commercial operators should consider $2 million.

## Mistake #4: Wrong Workers' Compensation Class Code

Workers' comp for spray foam contractors is more complicated than it looks, and the wrong class code can burn you in two directions.

Under-classification: If your employees are classified under a standard insulation installer class code that excludes spray foam operations, you may have workers' comp coverage that doesn't actually apply to SPF work. A claim for a worker injured during spray foam application could be denied on classification grounds.

Over-classification: Some agents default to higher-rate class codes that technically cover spray foam but dramatically overcharge your premium. The right code for your specific operations — open-cell residential, closed-cell commercial, roofing versus wall cavity — can make a significant difference in what you pay.

I've seen spray foam contractors overpaying their workers' comp by 40% because of a lazy class code assignment. I've also seen contractors with a code that doesn't actually cover their work.

**The fix:** Have your workers' comp class code reviewed by someone who understands spray foam operations. The correct code depends on the type of foam you spray, the substrates you work on, and the building types you enter. This isn't guesswork — it's something a specialist agent handles routinely.

## Mistake #5: No Commercial Auto Coverage for the Rig

Your personal auto policy doesn't cover your spray foam rig when it's in commercial use. Neither does a personal umbrella. If your rig is in an accident on the way to a job site, the commercial use exclusion in your personal auto policy will deny the claim — including liability for any damage or injuries caused to third parties.

This isn't a gray area. Commercial vehicles used for business purposes require commercial auto coverage. For spray foam contractors, that includes the truck, the trailer, and any mounted proportioner equipment — because the total value of a fully equipped SPF rig can exceed $150,000.

Beyond basic liability, you should think about: physical damage coverage (collision and comprehensive) for the rig itself, hired and non-owned auto liability if your employees ever drive personal vehicles for the business, and motor truck cargo if you transport chemicals or equipment for customers.

**The fix:** Make sure your vehicle fleet — every truck, van, and trailer used in business operations — is listed on a commercial auto policy. If you're using a personal vehicle for job site trips, talk to your agent about whether you need a commercial auto policy or a business use endorsement on your personal policy.
