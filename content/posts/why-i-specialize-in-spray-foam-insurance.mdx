Twenty years ago I was a generalist commercial insurance agent. Contractors, restaurants, retail shops, small manufacturers — I wrote it all. I was decent at it. But decent isn't good enough when a claim hits and the policy you thought covered you doesn't.

The moment that changed everything for me happened about fifteen years ago. A spray foam contractor I'd written a general liability policy for had a job go sideways. The foam went off-ratio — the A and B components mixed wrong — and the end result was soft, tacky foam that failed structurally. The homeowner filed a claim. My client had been in business for eight years. He did good work. He just had one bad batch on one bad day.

We filed the claim. The carrier denied it.

Why? The GL policy I'd placed had a standard faulty workmanship exclusion and a products-completed operations exclusion that, combined, wiped out any hope of coverage for that loss. I hadn't caught it. I didn't even know to look for it. I didn't understand what made spray foam different from every other contractor trade.

My client paid out of pocket. It nearly broke his business.

I never let that happen again.

## Going Deep Instead of Wide

After that claim, I spent the next two years doing nothing but learning spray foam. I talked to underwriters. I talked to contractors. I talked to claims adjusters who'd seen the losses. I learned what off-ratio foam actually means chemically, why it causes the kind of structural failures it does, and what insurance language you need in a policy to actually cover it.

I learned about the pollution exclusion and why it matters for spray foam specifically — because the chemicals in SPF systems can trigger the pollution exclusion in a standard GL policy, meaning a chemical exposure claim could be completely uninsured under a policy a generalist agent would consider "adequate."

I learned about the professional exclusion — why some GL carriers add language that excludes claims arising from any professional service, which can be used to deny a spray foam claim by arguing that diagnosing the correct mix ratio is a professional act.

I learned the class codes. Standard contractor class codes often exclude or severely limit spray foam work. I've seen contractors who thought they had workers' comp coverage find out during a claim that their insulation installer class code specifically excluded spray foam operations.

None of this is obvious. None of it is in the standard training an insurance agent gets. You learn it by getting deep in the weeds — or by watching a client get hurt.

## Why Specialization Protects You

Here's the honest truth about the insurance industry: most agents are generalists. They write homeowners, auto, and a smattering of commercial accounts. When a spray foam contractor comes to them, they pull up whatever GL product they normally use and add insulation contractors to the class. They get you a price. You buy it. Everyone feels good.

Until the claim.

I've talked to hundreds of spray foam contractors over the years. A significant number of them — when I ask to see their current policies — have coverage gaps that would leave them exposed on the most common claims in this trade. They don't know it. Their agent didn't tell them. Their agent didn't know.

I know. That's the only thing I do.

When I place a GL policy for a spray foam contractor, I'm checking for:

- **Off-ratio coverage endorsements** — does the policy actually cover faulty workmanship resulting from an improper mix ratio, or does the faulty workmanship exclusion nuke that?
- **Products-completed operations coverage** — is it included and does the language hold up for SPF claims?
- **Pollution exclusion carve-outs** — is there language that protects you on chemical exposure claims that a standard pollution exclusion would otherwise exclude?
- **Professional exclusion language** — does the policy have any professional services exclusion that could be used to deny a spray foam-related claim?

That's before I even look at workers' comp class codes, commercial auto, or contractor pollution liability.
